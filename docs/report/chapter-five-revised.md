# CHAPTER FIVE: SUMMARY, CONCLUSION AND RECOMMENDATIONS

## 5.1 Summary

This study set out to design, develop, and evaluate a digital system that helps Burundian refugees in Mahama find and assess information relevant to voluntary return. It began from the problem that return decisions depend on conditions that vary by destination and change over time, while the information available about those conditions is scattered, often undated, and difficult to judge. Requirements were gathered through an online questionnaire completed by 25 ALU students who live or have lived in Mahama, and the resulting system, Nuru, was built iteratively as a web application with a mobile prototype. Nuru organises return information by category and destination, displays each entry's source, source publication date, and last-checked date, links every source review to a specific version of an entry so that edited content cannot be published under an earlier approval, and allows the public to report problems that administrators must resolve with recorded notes. The system was evaluated through 30 automated tests, accessibility and responsiveness checks, measurements of the deployed API, and remote usability sessions with five participants.

## 5.2 Conclusion

The findings show that the problem identified in Chapter One is real for the participants in this study. Safety and changing local conditions were the information most needed, and outdated and conflicting information were the difficulties most often reported, which confirms that potential returnees need information whose source and currency they can judge. Participants valued visible sources and dates, preferred Kirundi and Kinyarwanda, and faced barriers of data cost, unreliable connectivity, and, for a fifth of them, lack of a suitable device. Nuru met these requirements in its functions: all automated tests passed, the publication rules worked as designed, the interface adapted to phone, tablet, and desktop screens, and accessibility scores reached 100 on the pages audited. Usability participants completed every task and rated the system positively.

The study's central expectation, that presenting return information with clear sources, dates, and a documented review process would help users not only find information but also judge it correctly, was only partly supported. Users could find information and identify its source and dates, but none of the five usability participants understood that a last-checked date does not confirm current conditions in a destination. The cause lay in the interface rather than in the underlying design: labels such as "Verified" told users more than the system could establish and, in some cases, contradicted the version-linked review. The study therefore concludes that source-attributed, version-checked content is a sound foundation for responsible return information, but that its value depends on how precisely the interface communicates what checking does and does not mean.

## 5.3 Recommendations

For the further development of Nuru, the most urgent change is to replace the manually set "Verified" badge and related wording with a "Source checked" label that is derived directly from the review of the current version, and to add a short notice on every entry explaining that a source check confirms that the entry matches its cited source and does not confirm conditions on the ground. The comprehension check should then be repeated to establish whether the change is effective. The usability findings also recommend clearer and less overlapping category labels, larger buttons, and a more visible reporting option, since these accounted for most of the help participants needed.

Because half of the participants preferred a messaging service and many faced high data costs, organisations considering a system of this kind should deliver its content through messaging channels and low-data formats alongside the website, rather than relying on a website alone. Any organisation that adopts Nuru should also assign named staff to review sources, respond to reports, and keep content current, because the system can enforce a review process but cannot supply the people who carry it out. Finally, Nuru should continue to direct users to UNHCR and other services for individual counselling, as participants themselves recognised when asked what to do with conflicting information.

## 5.4 Limitations

The study had several limitations that affect how far its findings can be generalised. Participants were ALU students with lived experience of Mahama rather than current residents recruited in the settlement, and most described themselves as very familiar with return information, so their needs, devices, and digital skills may differ from those of the wider refugee population. The samples were small, with 25 questionnaire respondents and five usability participants, below the planned six to eight and the guideline of 20 for acceptance testing. Because the approved ethics procedure rightly prohibited pressuring students to take part, recruitment depended entirely on volunteers, which limited numbers within the time available. The percentages reported should therefore be read as indicative. Usability testing used fictional content and a custom rating scale rather than the System Usability Scale, which prevents comparison with other systems.

Technical evaluation was also limited. Compatibility testing covered Chrome and the Android emulator only, the planned performance test under slow network conditions was not carried out, and automated tests ran against SQLite rather than the production PostgreSQL database. The mobile prototype was not validated on physical devices, translated content had not been reviewed by native speakers at scale, and three requirements were only partly met or deferred: a dedicated limitations notice, a separate archive state, and a list of entries due for review. Throughout the study, the researcher performed the administrator role, so the content review process has not yet been tested by an independent organisation.

## 5.5 Contributions to the Body of Knowledge

The study contributes context-specific evidence on the return information needs of people with lived experience of Mahama, a group on which little such research had been published. It also contributes a practical design for content governance in humanitarian information systems, in which approval is tied to an exact version of the content so that any substantive edit requires a new review. Its clearest contribution, however, is empirical evidence that trust labels can undermine the distinction a system is built to make: even with an accurate review process underneath, a "Verified" label led every usability participant to over-interpret what had been checked. This supports, with direct user evidence, the argument in the literature that trust signals and accuracy are separate concerns.

## 5.6 Suggestions for Further Studies

Future research should repeat the requirements gathering and usability evaluation with current residents of Mahama, recruited on site with appropriate permissions and language support, and with a larger sample that allows the use of the System Usability Scale and comparison across age, education, and digital confidence. A follow-up study should test whether revised labels and an explicit notice improve users' understanding of what a source check means, since this was the main weakness identified. Researchers or developers continuing this work should also explore delivering Nuru's content through messaging services and offline or low-data formats, measure performance under realistic network conditions, validate the mobile application on the devices participants actually use, and evaluate the content review process over several months with a partner organisation responsible for maintaining it. Such studies could examine whether access to source-checked information affects how people approach return decisions, a question this study did not attempt to answer.
