# CHAPTER TWO: LITERATURE REVIEW

## 2.1 Introduction

This chapter reviewed research and existing digital systems relevant to refugee information access, voluntary return, information credibility, and content management. It first defines the key concepts of the study and outlines the historical background of Burundian displacement and return. It then presents the theories that informed the study, reviews existing systems and related empirical research, and closes with general comments on the gap that the study addressed. Findings from other settings were treated as evidence to inform the investigation, while their applicability to Mahama remained a question for the study.

## 2.2 Conceptual Review

This section defines the five concepts on which the study was built. The way return-related information is organised and presented, including its sources, dates, categories, destinations, and language, was treated as the factor under the project's control, while users' ability to find, understand, and assess that information was treated as the outcome of interest.

**Voluntary return.** UNHCR (1996) defines voluntary repatriation as a return based on a free and informed decision, made in the absence of physical, psychological, or material pressure, and carried out in safety and dignity. The requirement that the decision be informed places access to accurate information at the centre of voluntariness. Conditions in the place of origin, particularly safety, weigh heavily in refugees' return intentions (Alrababah et al., 2023), alongside housing and property, livelihoods, services, and social connections (Alrababah & Casalis, 2024). Voluntary return therefore defined both the subject matter of Nuru and its boundary: the system was designed to support an informed decision, not to encourage, discourage, or make that decision.

**Information needs and information seeking.** An information need arises from a person's circumstances and leads to information seeking that can be helped or obstructed by personal, social, and environmental factors (Wilson, 1999). In the refugee context, Oduntan and Ruthven (2019) proposed a matrix that organises refugees' information needs across different areas of integration, showing that needs vary with a person's situation and stage.

**Information credibility.** Credibility is a judgement about the believability of information, centred on its trustworthiness and expertise, and internet users seldom carry out the thorough checks, such as confirming an author or date, that verification would require (Metzger, 2007). Instead, they rely on social and cognitive shortcuts, including reputation, endorsement by others, and consistency across sources (Metzger et al., 2010).

**Source checking and verification.** Refugees often face information that is unstable, incomplete, or difficult to confirm, a condition Wall et al. (2017) describe as information precarity, which leaves refugees vulnerable to misinformation and rumour. Refugee information platforms communicate trustworthiness to different degrees, but displaying trust signals does not by itself establish that content is accurate (Winkel & Friedrichs-Liesenkötter, 2022). The study therefore distinguished source checking, which records whether an entry accurately represents its cited source for a specific version of that entry, from verification of conditions on the ground, such as whether a destination is currently safe, which lies outside what the system can establish.

**Digital access and inclusion.** The digital divide is a sequence of gaps in motivation, physical access, digital skills, and actual use, rather than a simple question of whether a device is available (van Dijk, 2020). Refugees are considerably less likely than the general population to own an internet-capable phone, and cost is a major barrier to connectivity (UNHCR, 2016). For this reason, UNHCR (2022) emphasises combining digital channels with in-person services, and Madon and Schoemaker (2021) show that the benefits of humanitarian technology depend on the organisational arrangements surrounding its use.

## 2.3 Historical Background of the Research Topic

### 2.3.1 Origins and Early Context of the Research Problem

Displacement from Burundi has occurred in successive waves since independence. Mass violence in 1972 forced large numbers of Burundians into exile, many of whom settled in Tanzania and remained there for decades (Malkki, 1995). The assassination of President Melchior Ndadaye in 1993 triggered a civil war that caused further large-scale displacement (Lemarchand, 1996). Although the Arusha Peace and Reconciliation Agreement of 2000 and subsequent repatriation programmes enabled many refugees to return, the announcement in 2015 that President Pierre Nkurunziza would seek a third term led to protests, an attempted coup, and violence, and hundreds of thousands of people fled to neighbouring countries, including Rwanda (International Crisis Group, 2016).

Mahama camp was established in 2015 in Kirehe District, in Rwanda's Eastern Province, to receive refugees fleeing this crisis (UNHCR, 2023). In August 2020, UNHCR and the Governments of Rwanda and Burundi began facilitating voluntary repatriation from Rwanda, with the first group departing from Mahama (Sibomana, 2020). Because each wave of return took place under different conditions, and many potential returnees have spent years away from the places to which they may return, they cannot assume current conditions in specific destinations from past experience.

### 2.3.2 Foundational Theories

Research on how people seek information developed from early studies of library and system use. Wilson (1981) argued that the concept of an "information need" was too vague to study directly and proposed examining the information-seeking behaviour that arises from people's underlying needs and circumstances. Chatman (1996) later described the information poverty of people living as "outsiders", showing that mistrust and perceived risk can lead people to avoid seeking or sharing information even when it would help them. Lloyd et al. (2013) applied these ideas to refugees resettled in Australia, finding that refugees had to learn to navigate an unfamiliar information landscape and relied heavily on social and visual sources. Together, these studies established that information access depends on trust, social context, and familiarity with the information environment, not only on whether information exists.

### 2.3.3 Key Milestones

The spread of mobile phones changed how refugees obtain information. During the movement of refugees into Europe in 2015 and 2016, smartphones became essential tools for navigation, communication, and information, although they also exposed refugees to surveillance and unreliable information (Gillespie et al., 2018). Humanitarian organisations responded by developing dedicated digital information services, including Refugee.Info in Europe and Julisha.Info in Kenya, which combine published content with messaging channels (Julisha.Info, n.d.; Refugee.Info, n.d.). At an institutional level, UNHCR's (2022) Digital Transformation Strategy set out an approach that expands digital services while maintaining in-person support.

### 2.3.4 Historical Gaps and Transition to the Current Study

These milestones concern mainly refugees in transit, asylum seekers, and those integrating into host countries, and digital services have largely focused on rights and services in the place of refuge rather than on conditions in places of return. Within the literature reviewed, little attention had been given to how Burundian refugees in Rwanda find and assess destination-specific information about return, or to how a digital system might organise such information responsibly. The present study addressed this gap.

## 2.4 Theoretical Review

### 2.4.1 Foundational Theories

Three theories informed the design and evaluation of Nuru, each addressing a different stage of the study: understanding users' needs, presenting information in a way users can assess, and evaluating whether the resulting system was usable and useful.

**Wilson's model of information behaviour.** Wilson (1999) presents information behaviour as a set of nested activities: information searching forms part of information seeking, which in turn forms part of a person's wider information behaviour. An information need arises from a person's context, and intervening variables, including personal characteristics, social and interpersonal factors, environmental conditions, and the characteristics of information sources, determine whether that need leads to successful information seeking. This model guided the requirements stage: the questionnaire asked participants about the information people need, the sources they would consult, and the barriers, such as connectivity, data costs, and language, that could prevent them from using information.

**Credibility assessment.** Hilligoss and Rieh (2008) developed a framework of credibility assessment from a study of how 24 undergraduate students judged information in their everyday lives. It identifies three levels: the construct level, at which a person defines what credibility means to them; the heuristics level, at which they apply general rules of thumb; and the interaction level, at which they respond to specific cues from the content and its source. Because users depend on such cues rather than thorough verification (Metzger, 2007), Nuru displays each entry's cited source, the source's publication date, and the date of the last source check. The theory also informed the comprehension checks in usability testing, which examined whether participants interpreted these cues as intended.

**Technology Acceptance Model.** Davis (1989) proposed that acceptance of an information system depends mainly on perceived usefulness, the degree to which a person believes the system would help them, and perceived ease of use, the degree to which they believe using it would be free of effort. In this study, the model informed the usability evaluation, in which participants rated whether they could find information, whether labels were clear, whether the site explained its limitations, whether navigation worked well, and whether the reporting process was clear.

**Theoretical framework**

The three theories were combined into a framework that linked each stage of the study to a theoretical basis (Figure 2.1). Wilson's (1999) model framed the investigation of needs, sources, and barriers, which produced the requirements; credibility assessment theory (Hilligoss & Rieh, 2008; Metzger, 2007) shaped the presentation of sources, dates, and limitations; and the Technology Acceptance Model (Davis, 1989) shaped the evaluation of usefulness and ease of use. Together, the framework connected how information is organised and presented with the outcome the study sought to improve: users' ability to find, understand, and assess return-related information.

## 2.5 Overview of Existing Systems

This section examines Refugee.Info, Julisha.Info, and UNHCR's information services in relation to their intended users, channels, and relevance to voluntary return, and considers KoboToolbox separately as a supporting data collection technology. The comparison drew on published platform descriptions, which establish available functions but do not demonstrate effectiveness for users in Mahama.

**Refugee.Info.** Refugee.Info provides information services for refugees, migrants, and asylum seekers in European contexts, including Italy and Greece. It organises information around rights, administrative procedures, and services, offers multilingual content alongside communication through WhatsApp, Facebook Messenger, and Telegram, and in Greece includes information on assisted voluntary return and reintegration (Refugee.Info, n.d.). Organising content around practical questions provided a useful design reference for Nuru, although the service addresses different countries, procedures, and user populations.

**Julisha.Info.** Julisha.Info is a Signpost information service in Kenya for refugees, asylum seekers, and host communities. It offers information in English, Swahili, and Somali, accepts questions and feedback through its website, Facebook, WhatsApp, and SMS, and describes its content as responding to needs expressed by the communities it serves (Julisha.Info, n.d.). It illustrated the importance of investigating the channels and languages potential users already use, although its features do not establish which channels would be accessible in Mahama.

**UNHCR digital information services.** UNHCR Rwanda's Help website provides information on registration, documentation, health, legal assistance, work, and voluntary return, and its voluntary-return page directs refugees in Mahama to UNHCR staff at the Registration Centre for information and an interview (UNHCR, n.d.). It therefore connects public information with an established process for individual assistance, a principle reinforced by UNHCR's (2022) strategy. The page explains the process and relevant contacts but does not provide detailed profiles of return destinations, although such information may be available through counselling.

**KoboToolbox.** KoboToolbox supports the collection and analysis of research and operational data, including offline data collection on Android devices and through cached web forms (KoboToolbox, 2026). Its relevance to Nuru was mainly methodological, since collecting information does not establish whether it is accurate, current, or suitable for publication.

### 2.5.1 Strengths and Weaknesses of the Existing Systems

The reviewed systems showed how organised information and human support can work together, but none addressed return from Rwanda to particular destinations in Burundi, and their internal checking procedures were not documented in the sources examined. Table 2.1 summarises the comparison.

## 2.6 Review of Related Works

**Refugee information needs and information-seeking behaviour.** Oduntan and Ruthven (2021) used interviews to examine how refugees and asylum seekers obtained information during integration, finding that needs were met through both people, such as friends, solicitors, and caseworkers, and places, such as service providers and refugee-related institutions. Although their study concerned integration rather than return, it suggested that Nuru should link users to people who can explain information, because difficulty applying information may persist even when it is available online. Research among South Sudanese refugees in Uganda similarly showed that technological and social barriers affect how refugees seek and use information (Mukungu & Ngulube, 2026; Mukungu et al., 2026).

**Information credibility, trust, and verification.** Winkel and Friedrichs-Liesenkötter (2022) analysed information websites and applications for refugees in Germany and found that usability and trustworthiness were implemented to different degrees across platforms. Their analysis of platform features did not establish whether users actually trust the content, which suggested that perceived credibility and factual checking required separate attention in Nuru.

**Information and voluntary return decision-making.** Alrababah and Casalis (2024) reviewed empirical research on refugee return and identified security, economic opportunities, property rights, services, and social connections as influences, while noting that the effects of information gaps on return decisions require further investigation. Their review also found differing relationships between past exposure to violence and willingness to return, implying that information should let users examine their own concerns without assuming that the same conditions lead everyone to the same decision. A UNHCR account of returns from Mahama in 2020 provides a local illustration, in which one returnee described learning of peace in Burundi as part of her reason for returning (Sibomana, 2020), although it is a selected personal account rather than representative evidence.

**Digital technology in refugee contexts.** Madon and Schoemaker (2021) examined UNHCR's digital identity platform with refugees and organisational stakeholders in Bidi Bidi, Uganda, and found that improved service delivery depended on how technology interacted with organisational practices, incentives, and users' circumstances. Although digital identity differs from Nuru's purpose, the study showed why responsibilities for maintaining content and responding to errors deserve attention alongside software functions.

## 2.7 General Comments

The literature showed that refugees obtain information through social relationships, institutions, and digital channels, and that usability, credibility, and organisational responsibility all shape whether information is useful (Alrababah & Casalis, 2024; Oduntan & Ruthven, 2021; Winkel & Friedrichs-Liesenkötter, 2022). However, the return-related information needs and assessment practices of Burundian refugees in Mahama remained insufficiently established, and existing platforms did not show which functions would be useful in this setting. The study responded by gathering requirements from participants with lived experience of Mahama, and the review informed several design decisions: displaying each entry's source and dates, tying source checks to a specific content version, organising content by category and destination, providing Kirundi, Kinyarwanda, and Kiswahili, and linking users to support rather than presenting the platform as a substitute for counselling. The following chapter explains the methods used.
